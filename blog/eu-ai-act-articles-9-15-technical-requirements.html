Introduction: The Core Technical Obligations

Articles 9 through 15 of the EU AI Act define the technical and organizational obligations that apply to high-risk AI systems. These articles form the backbone of regulatory compliance for AI deployed in critical applications, from automated hiring decisions to autonomous vehicles.

This guide breaks down each article into engineering terms and practical implementation guidance. You will learn what each regulation requires, how to measure compliance, what checks AIR Blackbox runs, and how to implement these requirements in your codebase. This is not legal interpretation; this is engineering guidance.

The seven articles covered here represent the most technically dense requirements in the EU AI Act. Together, they establish a complete risk governance framework: Article 9 defines how you identify and manage risks, Article 10 ensures your training data is trustworthy, Article 11 documents everything, Article 12 logs everything tamper-proof, Article 14 puts humans in the loop, and Article 15 proves your system is robust. Article 13 (about transparency and provision of information to users) is outside the scope of this technical reference.

Article 9: Risk Management Systems

What the Regulation Says

Article 9 requires that you establish and implement a risk management system that covers the entire lifecycle of your high-risk AI system. This system must identify and analyze potential harms, put measures in place to mitigate those harms, and continuously monitor and re-evaluate risks as the system operates in the real world.

The regulation does not mandate a specific methodology. It does require that risk management be documented, systematic, and updated throughout the system's lifecycle. You must consider both the model itself and the application context.

What This Means in Code

In practice, Article 9 requires you to build risk awareness into your development pipeline. This includes:

  • Threat modeling: Identify ways your system could cause harm or be misused. Include both algorithmic failures (bias, adversarial inputs) and operational failures (dependency on stale data, monitoring gaps).
  • Risk classification: Document the severity and likelihood of each identified risk. This becomes your risk register.
  • Mitigation strategies: For each risk above your tolerance threshold, implement a control. This might be data quality checks, model performance thresholds, human review triggers, or architectural changes.
  • Monitoring and alerting: Establish metrics that detect when risks are materializing in production. Set up alerts that trigger investigation or intervention.
  • Lifecycle updates: Rerun risk analysis when you release new versions, when you detect distribution shift, or when you learn about a vulnerability.

AIR Blackbox Checks for Article 9

The AIR Blackbox scanner checks your code for Article 9 compliance by verifying:

  • Presence of risk assessment documentation in code comments or docstrings
  • Implementation of threat model-driven guard rails (input validation, output filtering)
  • Monitoring instrumentation that logs model predictions for audit
  • Exception handling that captures edge cases and failures
  • Evidence of performance tracking on protected characteristics (fairness metrics)
  • Presence of human review or override mechanisms before high-stakes actions

Article 10: Data and Data Governance

What the Regulation Says

Article 10 requires that the training data, validation data, and test data used for high-risk AI systems meet strict quality standards. You must document where your data came from, check it for bias and discrimination, and ensure it is representative of the populations and use cases your system will encounter.

This extends beyond the training phase: you must continue to assess data quality as you operate the system. If you detect that real-world data differs significantly from your training distribution, that is a red flag that your risk assessments need updating.

Technical Requirements

Article 10 breaks down into concrete technical obligations:

  • Data sourcing documentation: Record where each dataset came from, who collected it, how it was cleaned, and what exclusions were applied. This becomes your data sheet.
  • Bias testing: Measure performance separately for demographic groups protected under EU discrimination law. Identify disparities and document why they exist or how they are mitigated.
  • Data quality metrics: Define what "quality" means for your use case. This might include label agreement (inter-annotator agreement for human-labeled data), missing value rates, outlier detection, or freshness (how recently was the data collected).
  • Representativeness assessment: Compare the distribution of your training data to the distribution of users your system will serve. Document gaps and limitations.
  • Drift monitoring: In production, continuously compare incoming data to your training distribution. Set thresholds for when you must retrain or halt the system.

Code Example: Data Quality Assessment

import pandas as pd
from sklearn.metrics import confusion_matrix
import hashlib

class DataGovernance:
    def __init__(self):
        self.data_lineage = {}
        self.bias_report = {}
    
    def document_dataset(self, df, source, collection_date, annotators=None):
        """Record where data came from and how it was created."""
        data_hash = hashlib.sha256(
            pd.util.hash_pandas_object(df, index=True).values
        ).hexdigest()
        
        self.data_lineage[data_hash] = {
            "source": source,
            "collection_date": collection_date,
            "row_count": len(df),
            "missing_values": df.isnull().sum().to_dict(),
            "annotators": annotators,
            # This becomes your data sheet
        }
    
    def measure_fairness(self, y_true, y_pred, protected_attr):
        """Measure performance disparity across protected groups."""
        results = {}
        for group in protected_attr.unique():
            mask = protected_attr == group
            cm = confusion_matrix(
                y_true[mask], y_pred[mask]
            )
            # False positive rate for this group
            fpr = cm[0, 1] / (cm[0, 0] + cm[0, 1])
            results[group] = {"false_positive_rate": fpr}
        
        # Log the disparity for Article 10 documentation
        self.bias_report = results
        return results
    
    def check_drift(self, current_data, training_data, threshold=0.1):
        """Detect when live data diverges from training distribution."""
        # Compare distributions using statistical tests
        # If Kolmogorov-Smirnov test p-value < threshold, alert

            from scipy.stats import ks_2samp
            stat, p_value = ks_2samp(
                current_data[col],
                training_data[col]
            )
            if p_value < threshold:
                print(f"DRIFT DETECTED: {col} p-value={p_value}")

Article 11: Technical Documentation

What the Regulation Says

Article 11 requires comprehensive technical documentation of high-risk AI systems. This documentation must be sufficient for regulators, notified bodies (conformity assessors), and your own teams to understand how the system works, what assumptions it makes, and what its limitations are.

The regulation specifies that documentation must cover: the system's purpose and intended use, the algorithm or model architecture, the training process including datasets and parameters, the performance metrics and testing results, known limitations, and risk mitigation measures. This is the model card, the data sheet, and the architecture document combined.

Documentation Structure

In engineering terms, Article 11 requires four main documents:

  • Architecture and Algorithm Documentation: Explain the model type, layer-by-layer structure, mathematical formulation, and any novel techniques. Include diagrams and pseudocode.
  • Training Process Documentation: Describe hyperparameters, optimization algorithms, convergence criteria, and how you prevented overfitting. Include ablation studies showing which components matter most.
  • Evaluation and Performance Documentation: Report accuracy, fairness metrics, robustness tests, and edge case analysis. Include confusion matrices and per-group performance breakdowns.
  • Limitations and Failure Modes: Describe conditions under which the system performs poorly. Be explicit about the populations, environments, or input distributions where confidence is low.

Article 12: Record-Keeping and Automatic Logging

What the Regulation Says

Article 12 requires that high-risk AI systems automatically keep records of their operation. These records must be sufficient to demonstrate compliance with other articles and to enable effective monitoring and auditing. The regulation mandates a minimum retention period of three years for records of system operation and decisions.

Critically, these records must be tamper-proof. If a regulator or auditor requests logs, they must have confidence that the logs are genuine and unchanged since the system created them. This is where cryptographic techniques enter the picture.

What This Means in Code

Article 12 requires three technical capabilities:

  • Comprehensive logging: Record every input, every prediction, every override, and every error. Include metadata: user ID, timestamp, model version, confidence score.
  • Tamper-evidence: Use HMAC-SHA256 chains so that any modification to historical logs becomes immediately detectable. Each log entry signs the previous entry, creating an unbreakable chain.
  • Long-term retention: Store logs for at least three years. This typically means immutable storage (like AWS S3 with versioning disabled and delete protection enabled) or a dedicated audit database.

HMAC-SHA256 Tamper-Proof Chains

AIR Blackbox implements Article 12 compliance using HMAC-SHA256 chains, similar to blockchains but simpler and more efficient. Here is how it works:

When your AI system makes a decision, it creates a log entry with a cryptographic signature. The signature includes the hash of the previous entry, creating a chain. If anyone tries to modify an old entry, its signature will no longer match the next entry in the chain, and the tampering is immediately detected.

Code Example: Building Tamper-Proof Audit Chains

Python: HMAC-SHA256 audit chain implementation 
import hmac
import hashlib
import json
from datetime import datetime
import uuid

class TamperProofAuditLog:
    """Article 12: Implement tamper-proof logging with HMAC-SHA256 chains."""
    
    def __init__(self, secret_key):
        """Initialize with a secret key stored securely (e.g., AWS KMS)."""
        self.secret_key = secret_key.encode()
        self.chain = []
        self.last_signature = None
    
    def log_decision(self, event_type, model_name, input_data, output, 
                     user_id, confidence=None):
        """Log an AI system decision with tamper-proof signature."""
        entry = {
            "id": str(uuid.uuid4()),
            "timestamp": datetime.utcnow().isoformat(),
            "event_type": event_type,
            "model_version": model_name,
            "user_id": user_id,
            "input_hash": hashlib.sha256(
                json.dumps(input_data, sort_keys=True).encode()
            ).hexdigest(),
            "output": output,
            "confidence": confidence,
            "previous_signature": self.last_signature,
        }
        
        # Create HMAC signature over this entry
        entry_string = json.dumps(entry, sort_keys=True)
        signature = hmac.new(
            self.secret_key,
            entry_string.encode(),
            hashlib.sha256
        ).hexdigest()
        
        entry["signature"] = signature
        self.chain.append(entry)
        self.last_signature = signature
        
        return entry
    
    def verify_chain_integrity(self):
        """Detect if any log entry has been tampered with."""
        for i, entry in enumerate(self.chain):
            # Verify this entry's signature
            stored_signature = entry.pop("signature")
            entry_string = json.dumps(entry, sort_keys=True)
            expected_signature = hmac.new(
                self.secret_key,
                entry_string.encode(),
                hashlib.sha256
            ).hexdigest()
            
            if stored_signature != expected_signature:
                return False, f"Entry {i} has been modified"
            
            # Verify the chain link
            if i > 0:
                if entry["previous_signature"] != self.chain[i-1]["signature"]:
                    return False, f"Entry {i} is not linked correctly"
            
            entry["signature"] = stored_signature  # Restore for storage
        
        return True, "Chain is intact"

# Usage
logger = TamperProofAuditLog(secret_key="your-secret-from-kms")

# Every decision gets logged with a signature
logger.log_decision(
    event_type="loan_approval_decision",
    model_name="CreditRisk-v2.1",
    input_data={"credit_score": 750, "income": 80000},
    output="APPROVED",
    user_id="agent-001",
    confidence=0.92
)

# Verify integrity before submitting to regulator
is_valid, message = logger.verify_chain_integrity()
print(f"Audit log valid: {is_valid} - {message}")

This approach ensures that regulators can verify the authenticity of your logs. The chain makes tampering obvious: if you change a decision from 2024, the signature of that entry will no longer match the 2025 entry that links to it.

Article 14: Human Oversight

What the Regulation Says

Article 14 requires that high-risk AI systems be designed to enable human oversight of automated decisions. The regulation recognizes that humans are the real decision-makers; AI is a tool that humans should use with proper safeguards.

This means your system must be designed so that humans can understand what the AI recommends, why it recommends it, and can override or reject the recommendation. The human must have the authority, the information, and the technical capability to intervene.

Technical Requirements for Human-in-the-Loop

Article 14 breaks down into concrete technical obligations:

  • Explainability: Humans must understand the reasoning behind each decision. This means feature importance, rule extraction, attention mechanisms, or other interpretability techniques relevant to your model type.
  • Confidence thresholds: For low-confidence predictions, trigger automatic human review. The human reviews the case, asks questions, and makes the final decision.
  • Override mechanisms: Humans must be able to reject or modify the AI's recommendation. This decision must be logged and must be final (the AI does not override the human).
  • Monitoring and escalation: Track how often humans override the AI. If override rates are high, that signals the AI is not trustworthy and needs retraining.
  • Access controls: Humans who have the authority to override must be identified and authenticated. Their overrides must be logged with their identity.

Code Example: AIR Blackbox Gate for Human Oversight

Python: Implementing human review gates 
from enum import Enum
from datetime import datetime
import logging

class DecisionGate(Enum):
    APPROVED = "approved"
    HELD_FOR_REVIEW = "held_for_review"
    REJECTED = "rejected"

class HumanOversightGate:
    """Article 14: Require human review for high-risk decisions."""
    
    def __init__(self, confidence_threshold=0.85):
        self.confidence_threshold = confidence_threshold
        self.review_queue = []
        self.override_log = []
        self.logger = logging.getLogger(__name__)
    
    def evaluate_decision(self, model_output, confidence, 
                         feature_importance, case_id):
        """Determine if a decision needs human review."""
        
        # Low confidence is a red flag
        if confidence < self.confidence_threshold:
            return self.require_review(
                case_id, model_output, confidence, 
                reason=f"Confidence {confidence} below threshold {self.confidence_threshold}",
                explanation=feature_importance
            )
        
        # High stakes always go to review (could be parameterized)
        return DecisionGate.APPROVED
    
    def require_review(self, case_id, recommendation, confidence, 
                      reason, explanation):
        """Queue a decision for human review."""
        review_item = {
            "case_id": case_id,
            "recommendation": recommendation,
            "confidence": confidence,
            "reason_for_review": reason,
            "explanation": explanation,
            "created_at": datetime.utcnow().isoformat(),
            "reviewed": False,
            "reviewer_id": None,
            "final_decision": None,
        }
        
        self.review_queue.append(review_item)
        self.logger.info(f"Case {case_id} requires human review: {reason}")
        return DecisionGate.HELD_FOR_REVIEW
    
    def human_review(self, case_id, reviewer_id, final_decision, notes):
        """Human makes the final decision (AI recommendation is not mandatory)."""
        review_item = next(
            (item for item in self.review_queue 
             if item["case_id"] == case_id),
            None
        )
        
        if not review_item:
            raise ValueError(f"Case {case_id} not found in review queue")
        
        review_item["reviewed"] = True
        review_item["reviewer_id"] = reviewer_id
        review_item["final_decision"] = final_decision
        review_item["reviewer_notes"] = notes
        review_item["reviewed_at"] = datetime.utcnow().isoformat()
        
        # Log if human overrode the AI
        if final_decision != review_item["recommendation"]:
            self.override_log.append({
                "case_id": case_id,
                "ai_recommendation": review_item["recommendation"],
                "human_decision": final_decision,
                "reviewer_id": reviewer_id,
                "timestamp": review_item["reviewed_at"],
            })
            self.logger.warning(
                f"Case {case_id}: Human overrode AI recommendation "
                f"({review_item['recommendation']} -> {final_decision})"
            )
        
        return final_decision
    
    def get_override_rate(self):
        """Monitor how often humans disagree with the AI."""
        if not self.override_log:
            return 0.0
        
        total_reviewed = len([item for item in self.review_queue 
                             if item["reviewed"]])
        overrides = len(self.override_log)
        
        return (overrides / total_reviewed * 100) if total_reviewed > 0 else 0

# Usage
gate = HumanOversightGate(confidence_threshold=0.80)

# AI makes recommendation
recommendation, confidence, importance = model.predict(
    features, return_confidence=True, return_feature_importance=True
)

# Gate checks if review is needed
status = gate.evaluate_decision(
    model_output=recommendation,
    confidence=confidence,
    feature_importance=importance,
    case_id="loan-12345"
)

if status == DecisionGate.HELD_FOR_REVIEW:
    # Human reviews the case in the queue
    human_decision = gate.human_review(
        case_id="loan-12345",
        reviewer_id="alice@company.com",
        final_decision="APPROVED",
        notes="Applicant has strong credit history despite low score"
    )
    print(f"Final decision: {human_decision}")
    
# Monitor if AI is trustworthy
override_rate = gate.get_override_rate()
if override_rate > 15:  # If humans override more than 15% of the time
    print(f"WARNING: Override rate {override_rate}% is high. Model needs retraining.")

This implementation ensures humans are in control: they review low-confidence cases, they can override the AI, and you track whether the AI is actually helping (low override rate) or creating work (high override rate).

Article 15: Accuracy, Robustness and Cybersecurity

What the Regulation Says

Article 15 requires high-risk AI systems to achieve and maintain an appropriate level of accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity. This is the quality and security requirement: your AI must work correctly under normal conditions, must fail gracefully under adversarial conditions, and must be protected against attack and misuse.

The regulation does not specify exact accuracy thresholds (these depend on the use case), but it requires you to measure accuracy, document it, test robustness, and implement security controls. These controls include adversarial testing, prompt injection defense for language models, and vulnerability scanning for the system as a whole.

Three Pillars of Article 15

Article 15 breaks down into three technical areas:

  • Accuracy and Performance: Measure prediction accuracy on held-out test data. Break this down by subgroups to ensure performance is equitable. Set a performance floor: if accuracy drops below this level, the system should not operate.
  • Robustness Testing: Test how the system responds to adversarial inputs, distribution shift, missing data, or boundary conditions. Adversarial robustness means the system cannot be easily manipulated by small, carefully-crafted input changes.
  • Cybersecurity: Protect the system from unauthorized access, injection attacks, and data poisoning. This includes prompt injection defense for language models, input validation for all systems, and secure access controls.

Prompt Injection and Adversarial Robustness

For language model agents, robustness means defending against prompt injection. Attackers embed hidden instructions in user input, hoping the model will follow them instead of its intended task. Article 15 requires you to test for this vulnerability and implement defenses.

AIR Blackbox includes a prompt injection scanner that flags vulnerable patterns in your code, such as directly concatenating user input into prompts without sanitization or instruction framing.

Code Example: Testing Robustness and Injection Defense

Python: Article 15 robustness and security checks 
import numpy as np
from air_blackbox import check_prompt_injection, scan_code

class RobustnessTest:
    """Article 15: Test accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity."""
    
    def __init__(self, model, X_test, y_test):
        self.model = model
        self.X_test = X_test
        self.y_test = y_test
        self.results = {}
    
    def test_accuracy(self):
        """Baseline performance on clean test data."""
        predictions = self.model.predict(self.X_test)
        accuracy = (predictions == self.y_test).mean()
        self.results["accuracy"] = accuracy
        return accuracy
    
    def test_adversarial_robustness(self, perturbation_fraction=0.1):
        """Test accuracy under adversarial input perturbation."""
        # Add small random noise to inputs
        X_perturbed = self.X_test + np.random.normal(
            0, perturbation_fraction, self.X_test.shape
        )
        
        predictions = self.model.predict(X_perturbed)
        adversarial_accuracy = (predictions == self.y_test).mean()
        
        # Article 15 requires documenting the drop
        robustness_drop = self.results["accuracy"] - adversarial_accuracy
        self.results["adversarial_accuracy"] = adversarial_accuracy
        self.results["robustness_drop"] = robustness_drop
        
        if robustness_drop > 0.15:  # If accuracy drops > 15%, that is a problem
            print(
                f"WARNING: Robustness issue detected. "
                f"Accuracy dropped {robustness_drop*100:.1f}% under perturbation."
            )
        
        return adversarial_accuracy
    
    def test_edge_cases(self):
        """Test with boundary inputs and missing data."""
        edge_cases = {
            "all_zeros": np.zeros_like(self.X_test[:1]),
            "all_ones": np.ones_like(self.X_test[:1]),
            "max_values": np.max(self.X_test) * np.ones_like(self.X_test[:1]),
            "min_values": np.min(self.X_test) * np.ones_like(self.X_test[:1]),
        }
        
        for case_name, case_input in edge_cases.items():
            try:
                output = self.model.predict(case_input)
                self.results[f"edge_case_{case_name}"] = "handled"
            except Exception as e:
                self.results[f"edge_case_{case_name}"] = f"error: {str(e)}"
                print(f"Edge case {case_name} caused error: {e}")

How These Articles Work Together: The Compliance Picture

Articles 9 through 15 form a complete governance framework. They are not independent requirements; they work together to establish end-to-end accountability:

Article Responsibility Outcome
Article 9 Identify and manage risks Risk register with mitigation strategies
Article 10 Ensure training data is trustworthy Data sheet with bias testing and representativeness
Article 11 Document the system comprehensively Technical documentation and model card
Article 12 Keep tamper-proof records HMAC-SHA256 audit trail, 3-year retention
Article 14 Enable human oversight Review gates, override mechanisms, monitoring
Article 15 Ensure accuracy, robustness, and security Performance metrics, adversarial testing, injection defense

Think of Article 9 as the foundation: you identify what can go wrong (risks). Articles 10 and 11 build the system safely: you use trusted data and document everything. Article 12 adds accountability: every decision is logged immutably. Article 14 ensures control: humans are in the loop. Article 15 ensures quality: the system is accurate, robust, and secure.

When a regulator audits your system, they will ask:

  • Show me your risk analysis (Article 9). Do you have a risk register? What are your mitigation strategies?
  • Show me your data documentation (Article 10). Where did your training data come from? Did you test for bias?
  • Show me your technical documentation (Article 11). Can I understand your system from your documentation?
  • Show me your audit logs (Article 12). Can I see a complete record of system operation and verify it has not been tampered with?
  • Show me how humans are in the loop (Article 14). When does the system require human review? Can humans override?
  • Show me your robustness testing (Article 15). How accurate is your system? How do you defend against attacks?

This is the compliance picture: six connected requirements that together establish accountability and oversight of high-risk AI systems.

Standards Crosswalk: EU AI Act to ISO 42001 and NIST AI RMF

The EU AI Act is not the only standard you may need to follow. If you operate internationally or in regulated sectors, you may also need to comply with ISO 42001 (AI Management System) or NIST AI Risk Management Framework. The good news: these standards align closely with Articles 9 through 15.

EU AI Act Article ISO 42001 Section NIST AI RMF Function
Article 9: Risk Management Clause 8.2: Risk management GOVERN: Risk management framework
Article 10: Data Governance Clause 8.4: Data management MEASURE: Data quality assessment
Article 11: Documentation Clause 8.3: Model management MEASURE: Model documentation
Article 12: Audit Logs Clause 8.5: Monitoring GOVERN: Audit and accountability
Article 14: Human Oversight Clause 8.6: Human oversight GOVERN: Human review mechanisms
Article 15: Robustness Clause 8.7: Performance evaluation MEASURE: Performance and robustness testing

If you implement the Article 9-15 requirements correctly, you are most of the way to ISO 42001 and NIST AI RMF compliance. The documents you create (risk register, data sheet, technical documentation, audit logs) become artifacts for all three frameworks.
